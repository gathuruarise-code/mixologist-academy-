Part 1

Introduction to Mixology & Bartending

1. Mixologist

A mixologist is a professional who specializes in creating, developing and preparing alcoholic and non-alcoholic drinks by combining ingredients, spirits, flavours and techniques. A mixologist understands beverages, recipes, flavour balance, presentation, garnishing and bar equipment.

Example: Creating a Blue Lagoon using vodka, blue curaçao, lemonade and ice.

2. Bartending

Bartending is the profession of preparing, mixing and serving alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages in bars, hotels, restaurants, clubs and events. Duties include taking orders, preparing drinks, serving customers, maintaining cleanliness, handling payments, monitoring stock and serving alcohol responsibly.

3. Difference between a bartender and a mixologist

BartenderMixologist
Prepares and serves drinks.Specializes in creating and developing drinks.
Focuses strongly on service and bar operations.Focuses strongly on flavour, ingredients and innovation.
Often follows established recipes.May modify or develop recipes.
Manages customer orders and workflow.Studies combinations, techniques and presentation.

A professional can be both a bartender and a mixologist.

Part 2

Bartender Basics

Types of bartenders

  1. Hotel bartender: Works in hotel bars, restaurants and lounges.
  2. Restaurant bartender: Prepares drinks for restaurant guests.
  3. Cocktail bartender: Specializes in cocktails.
  4. Flair bartender: Performs bottle and shaker tricks while entertaining guests.
  5. Mobile bartender: Provides bar services at private events.
  6. Club bartender: Works in nightclubs and entertainment venues.
  7. Service bartender: Prepares drinks for waitstaff to deliver.
  8. Head bartender: Supervises staff and bar operations.
  9. Banquet bartender: Serves at weddings, conferences and functions.
  10. Barista-bartender: Works with coffee and alcoholic beverages.

What does a mixologist do?

  • Creates and tests cocktail recipes.
  • Studies spirits, liqueurs, mixers and flavour profiles.
  • Balances sweet, sour, bitter and strong flavours.
  • Develops menus and recommends glassware.
  • Garnishes drinks and maintains consistency.
  • Trains staff and helps control beverage quality.

Qualities of a good bartender/mixologist

Communication and customer service
Honesty and responsibility
Creativity and product knowledge
Patience and good memory
Speed with accuracy
Hygiene and attention to detail
Teamwork and confidence
Ability to work under pressure

Core responsibilities

  1. Maintain personal and workplace hygiene.
  2. Know products and recipes.
  3. Measure ingredients accurately.
  4. Prepare and present drinks consistently.
  5. Manage stock and reduce waste.
  6. Verify age and follow responsible-service rules.

Part 6

Beverages: Classification

Non-alcoholic beverages

Examples include water, tea, coffee, fresh juices, soft drinks, energy drinks, mocktails, milkshakes and smoothies.

Alcoholic beverages

Fermented

Yeast converts sugars into alcohol. Examples: wine, beer and cider.

Distilled spirits

Fermented liquid is distilled to concentrate alcohol. Examples: whiskey, gin, vodka, tequila, brandy and rum.

Fortified wines

Wine with added distilled spirit. Examples: Port, Sherry, Madeira and Marsala.

ABV means alcohol by volume: the percentage of a drink's volume that is pure alcohol.

Tequila

Introduction

Tequila is a Mexican distilled spirit made from sugars of the Blue Weber agave plant. It is produced in designated areas of Mexico, with Jalisco being the main production region. Typical strength is around 35–55% ABV depending on product and market.

Types

  • Blanco / Silver: Usually unaged or rested briefly; clear.
  • Reposado: Aged in oak for at least two months but less than one year.
  • Añejo: Aged for at least one year but less than three years.
  • Extra Añejo: Aged for at least three years.
  • Joven / Gold: May be a blend or a Blanco with permitted colour/flavour adjustments.

Production process

  1. Harvest: Mature Blue Weber agave is harvested; the heart is called a piña.
  2. Cooking: Piñas are cooked to make sugars fermentable.
  3. Extraction: Cooked agave is crushed to obtain juice.
  4. Fermentation: Yeast converts sugars into alcohol.
  5. Distillation: The fermented liquid is distilled, commonly twice.
  6. Ageing: The spirit is rested or aged according to the style.
  7. Bottling: It may be filtered, diluted to bottling strength and bottled.

Whiskey / Whisky

Introduction

Whiskey is a distilled alcoholic beverage made from fermented grain mash and matured in wooden casks as required by its category. Grains include barley, corn, rye and wheat. Typical bottled strength is around 40–50% ABV.

Types

  • Scotch whisky: Made in Scotland under Scotch whisky rules.
  • Irish whiskey: Made in Ireland under Irish whiskey rules.
  • Bourbon: American whiskey made from a mash containing at least 51% corn and meeting legal production requirements.
  • Rye whiskey: Made with a legally specified high proportion of rye.
  • Tennessee whiskey: Tennessee-produced whiskey associated with charcoal mellowing requirements.
  • Japanese whisky: Whisky produced in Japan under applicable standards.
  • Canadian whisky: Produced in Canada, often blended.

Production process

  1. Malting: Barley is soaked, germinated and dried to develop enzymes.
  2. Mashing: Grains are milled and mixed with hot water to extract sugars.
  3. Fermentation: Yeast converts sugars into alcohol.
  4. Distillation: Alcohol is concentrated in pot or column stills.
  5. Maturation: Spirit is aged in wooden casks according to the category's rules.
  6. Blending: Different whiskies may be combined for consistency.
  7. Bottling: It is brought to bottling strength, sometimes filtered, then bottled.

Gin

Introduction

Gin is a distilled spirit whose predominant characteristic flavour comes from juniper. Other botanicals can include coriander seed, citrus peel, angelica root and spices. Common bottled strength is around 37.5–47% ABV, depending on the product and market.

Types

  • London Dry: Dry style with juniper-forward flavour and strict production rules; it need not be made in London.
  • Plymouth: Protected geographical style from Plymouth, England.
  • Old Tom: Traditionally sweeter style.
  • Genever: Traditional juniper spirit associated with the Netherlands and Belgium.
  • Contemporary / New Western: Gives notable emphasis to botanicals beyond juniper.
  • Flavoured gin: Gin with additional flavours.

Production process

  1. Prepare a suitable neutral base spirit.
  2. Select juniper and other botanicals.
  3. Extract flavour by steeping botanicals or passing alcohol vapour through them.
  4. Distil as required by the gin style.
  5. Dilute with water to the required strength.
  6. Filter where appropriate and bottle.

Vodka

Introduction

Vodka is a distilled spirit traditionally made from fermented grains or potatoes, though other agricultural materials may be used. It is generally relatively neutral in flavour. Typical bottled strength is around 40% ABV.

Types

  • Plain / unflavoured vodka.
  • Flavoured vodka.
  • Fruit-infused vodka.
  • Grain vodka.
  • Potato vodka.

Production process

  1. Select grain, potatoes or another suitable agricultural material.
  2. Prepare the material so its starches can be converted into fermentable sugars.
  3. Ferment with yeast.
  4. Distil to concentrate alcohol.
  5. Filter where appropriate.
  6. Dilute with water to the intended bottling strength.
  7. Bottle the finished vodka.

Part 11 • Beverage study

Wine

Introduction

Wine is an alcoholic beverage produced by fermenting grape juice. It is generally not distilled. Many wines fall around 8–15% ABV, although strengths vary.

Types of wine

  • Red: Usually fermented with dark grape skins, contributing colour and tannin. Examples: Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Pinot Noir and Shiraz/Syrah.
  • White: Usually made with limited skin contact. Examples: Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling and Pinot Grigio.
  • Rosé: Made with limited contact between red grape skins and juice.
  • Sparkling: Contains carbon dioxide. Examples: Champagne, Prosecco and Cava.
  • Dessert: Sweet wine commonly served with or after dessert.
  • Fortified: Wine with added distilled spirit. Examples: Port, Sherry, Madeira and Marsala.

Production process

  1. Harvest grapes at suitable ripeness.
  2. Sort grapes and remove unwanted material.
  3. Crush and, where appropriate, destem grapes.
  4. Ferment grape juice with yeast.
  5. Press at the appropriate stage to separate juice from solids.
  6. Clarify to remove unwanted particles.
  7. Age in stainless steel, oak or another suitable vessel.
  8. Blend where desired, then bottle.

Production order differs: white wines are commonly pressed before fermentation, while red wines commonly ferment with skins before pressing.

Brandy

Introduction

Brandy is a distilled alcoholic beverage made from fermented fruit juice, most commonly grapes. Grape brandy is often made by distilling wine. Typical strength varies, commonly around 35–60% ABV.

Types

  • Cognac: Protected grape brandy from the Cognac region of France.
  • Armagnac: Protected grape brandy from the Armagnac region of France.
  • Fruit brandy: Made from fruits such as apples, pears or cherries.
  • Grape brandy: Made by distilling grape wine.
  • Pisco: Grape spirit produced in Peru and Chile.
  • Calvados: Protected apple or apple-and-pear brandy from Normandy, France.

Cognac ageing classifications

  • VS: Youngest eau-de-vie aged at least two years.
  • VSOP: Youngest eau-de-vie aged at least four years.
  • XO: Youngest eau-de-vie aged at least ten years.

Production process

  1. Select and prepare fruit.
  2. Crush fruit and extract juice.
  3. Ferment juice into a wine or fruit wash.
  4. Distil the fermented liquid.
  5. Age in oak barrels where appropriate.
  6. Blend different batches or ages where desired.
  7. Adjust to bottling strength and bottle.

Standard cocktail preparation procedure

  1. Read and confirm the recipe.
  2. Select the correct glass.
  3. Prepare the garnish.
  4. Collect ingredients and tools.
  5. Measure ingredients with a jigger.

  9. Garnish appropriately.
  10. Wipe the glass and serve.

Part 14

Bar Hygiene & Safety

  • Wash hands regularly and maintain personal hygiene.
  • Clean and sanitize tools and work surfaces.
  • Never scoop ice with a drinking glass.
  • Use clean ice scoops and tongs; store the scoop hygienically.
  • Store garnishes safely and use clean utensils.
  • Label and date opened ingredients where required.
  • Keep raw food separate from ready-to-eat garnishes.
  • Dispose of broken glass safely; never pick shards up by hand.
  • Follow local age-verification and alcohol-service laws.
  • Do not serve more alcohol to a visibly intoxicated customer; follow venue policy and local law.
